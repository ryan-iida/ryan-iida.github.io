Kurokawa's Capsule Tower—Giving Form to a Changing Landscape

The demolition of one of Tokyo’s only remaining relics of the Metabolist movement, The Nakagin Capsule Tower, sadly surprised no one when it began on April 12th, 2022. The building stood neglected for 15 years after its demolition was decided in 2007, acting as a reminder of an ambition left unachieved. The Tower’s decrepit state should not be the way in which the building is remembered, however; it should instead be remembered for the intentions its architect, Kisho Kurokawa, had for Japan following World War II. Kurokawa’s official career may have began with the creation of the Capsule Tower, but the building was a culmination of teachings gathered throughout the architect’s time as a student of Kenzo Tange and as a founding member of the Metabolist group. Working alongside like-minded students of Tange in the Tange Lab, Kurokawa would come to form Metabolism, an architectural ideology modeling buildings after the human biological systems as a solution to Japan’s unsteady conditions in the 1950’s and 60’s. The precepts of the Metabolist group would take shape in the modular and regenerative construction of the Nakagin Capsule Tower, the world's first instance of “capsule architecture.” Made possible by the use of prefabricated design, the building is an example of how architectural design could promote a lifestyle necessary for a period of reconstitution in social order. The Nakagin Capsule Tower gave shape to an architectural form that emulated the dynamic nature of Japanese society to adapt the country for the postwar era.

“War helped me discover Japanese culture”1

The turbulence elicited by Japan’s reconstruction after World War II provoked the formation of the Metabolist Movement as a means of adapting the Japanese identity for the postwar era, as Kisho Kurokawa writes it. Kurokawa’s autobiography on his career and time as a member of the Metabolist group, Metabolism in Architecture, begins with him looking upon the scorched remains of Nagoya, his home town, after the firebombings of World War II. This, in a sense, was the state of Japan after the war. Due to the wooden construction of Japan’s buildings, Japanese cities were burnt to the ground during the destruction of the war, requiring a complete rebuilding effort on a national scale.2 Miraculously, however, Japan saw a decade of economic prosperity during the 1950’s as a result of American occupation and the use of Japanese factories during the Korean War. This economic revival upset the once stagnant social order of the country and called for a reshaping of the urban landscape; the expansion of the tertiary sector and the flourishing of new social and artistic movements brought with them a constructive confusion as to how the buildings of Japan should adapt to these changes. Kurokawa and the soon-to-be Metabolists would set themselves apart from their contemporaries as they “found it meaningless” to attempt to revive Japan by use of monuments and static imagery in architecture; instead, they found an opportunity amongst the turmoil—a chance to propel Japan forward by means of a new architectural philosophy, one that separated itself from that which was destroyed.3

At the momentous 1960 World Design Conference in Tokyo, Kiyonori Kikutake, Fumihiko Maki, Noboru Kawazoe, and Kisho Kurokawa would form the Metabolist Group, advocating for a necessary architectural shift towards a coexistence between humanity and technology. Within Metabolism 1960- a Proposal for a New Urbanism, the group’s founding prospectus, the use of technology as a catalyst for society’s homeostatic maintenance is advocated for eagerly. The group explains that architectural design and technology should point towards “human vitality” with technology being an “extension of humanity;” furthermore, they declare an aim to “encourage the active metabolic development” of Japanese society through their creations.4 In essence, the structures that humans interact with on a daily basis should logically reflect the nature of the human body and spirit. And, the development of an architectural language that reflects the human homeostatic devices through the use of adaptable technology would achieve this aim. This tenet would take the form of buildings bred to sustain the fluctuations of Japanese history and develop amidst times of economic prosperity. The Metabolists believed that these living buildings would act as conduits towards Japan’s progression from its postwar state—a declaration that would be acted upon in the regenerative, adaptable, and expressive architecture of the Metabolist movement.

“This building is not an apartment house”5

Standing in the heart of the bustling Ginza district, towards Tokyo’s urban center, is the Nakagin Capsule Tower, which completed construction in 1972 (see Figure 1). With two steel-reinforced concrete towers acting as a foundation, the tower’s unique facade is constituted by 144 “capsule” units that jut out in a “seemingly random pattern, suggesting an on-going process,” as Metabolist Scholar Zhongjie Lin denotes.6 What makes the building unique is its variable nature and prefabricated approach to construction; each capsule is fabricated independently of the rest of the building and then attached to a tower by bolts, meaning each unit could be removed and replaced without a major renovation.7 This approach contributed to the building’s worldwide acclaim for being assembled in just 30 days despite beginning manufacturing in 1970.8 A dissection of the tower’s anatomy, a composition of coexisting systems, reveals the Nakagin Capsule Tower’s organic quality through its use of “metabolic cycles;” the permanent towers and moveable capsules live independently of one another, with the latter being designed to be replaced and refitted every 30 years given changes in the lives of the building’s inhabitants.9 The driving force behind the dynamic architecture of Kurokawa’s creation was a careful consideration of the lifestyle of an emerging class of people in the wake of the postwar period of Japan. Commissioned by the Nakagin Company real estate firm, Kisho Kurokawa aimed to create housing for the “homo movens,” as he called them—people on the move.10

The modularity of the prefabricated units within the Nakagin Capsule Tower was planned as an opportunity for Japan’s burgeoning population of working people to express an identity once obscured by the typical apartment building. Due to the increasing congestion of Tokyo’s center as a result of the sharp population growth seen after the war, Tokyo’s residents steadily drifted towards the outskirts of the region in search of more affordable and spacious housing. Meanwhile, Japan’s economic boom brought with it an influx of professional working people with jobs located in Tokyo, creating a dilemma in which some 3 million people commuted at least an hour into the city everyday.11 Kurokawa was commissioned to create an in-between home of sorts for such people—a place for business people to stay overnight or for professionals requiring an atelier. Thus, an office desk, radio, telephone, and other amenities necessary for business travel came integrated into the interior of each capsule (see Figure 2). Each one was unique, however, fitting the tastes and eccentricities of its residents; buyers had the option to customize their environments, choosing material finishes, color schemes, and auxiliary equipment.12 This aspect ran in stark contrast to the “anonymous and impersonal” nature of the apartment building, as Kurokawa himself argues; standard apartment buildings created a disconnect between their internal environments and their residents as a result of the effort to house as many people as possible through uniform construction, effectively burying residents’ identity within their form.13 Made possible by the technology of prefabrication, the ability to construct one’s environment was a novel opportunity to give an architectural form to the dynamic lifestyles of Japan’s expanding professional class. Kurokawa explains this quite poetically: “The Nakagin Capsule Tower is not strictly an architectural expression of an apartment house, it is an expression of the 144 people who reside in its 144 units.”14

The Tower’s regenerative maintenance was proposed as a way to preserve the social significance of Tokyo’s buildings even as the urban landscape continuously developed. Amidst Japan’s reconstruction, Kisho Kurokawa and the Metabolists noticed a wasteful tendency in which advancement overruled sentimentality; the rapid obsolescence of once new buildings and the failure of single parts of whole structures rashly led to the demolition of entire buildings—a cycle which stripped Tokyo’s buildings of their social significance.15 This process was repeated to the point where it became common knowledge that Japan’s buildings were built not to stand the test of time, but instead to fit their current environment and be replaced soon after.16 Acknowledging the need for innovation yet dissatisfied with this process, the architect created the Nakagin Capsule Tower with the hopes of demonstrating how a city that continuously renewed itself could still maintain its original form, preserving the impetus behind its creation and its significance to the local culture.17 The detachable units of the Capsule Tower made it possible to renovate the building without shutting down its entire function or demolishing it altogether. Contrary to the stagnant conditions of the monuments that the Metabolists wished to separate themselves from, the Nakagin Capsule Tower was proposed to be an active memory—a living testament to the state of Tokyo in the past, present, and future. In coming to terms with the impermanence of architecture, Kurokawa applied a philosophy of dynamism as a conduit for preservation; in continuously adapting to promote the advancement of Tokyo and its people, the Nakagin Capsule Tower worked to prolong its life amongst the unsteady cityscape it was erected in, a symbolic struggle representative of Japan’s steadfast progression from its once destroyed state.

“To create a new Japan”18

The Nakagin Capsule Tower was a victim of the very process it aimed to amend. Due to its decreasing popularity and atrophying concrete structure, the building was slated for demolition in 2007 and set to be replaced by a building featuring 60 percent more square footage. In an effort to save his creation, Kurokawa sought support from major architectural organizations in Japan for a necessary repair of the building’s two towers and a refitting with improved capsules, a fulfillment of his original plan for the Tower. Despite the overwhelming and essentially-unanimous support, the plan was never executed, most likely due to Kurokawa’s sudden death in the same year due to congestive heart failure.19 The inevitability of the process Kurokawa foresaw during the building’s conception is largely due to the exorbitant prices of land in Japan, a country whose buildings cost an average of 10 percent of the value of the land on which they sit.20 As a result, buildings deemed unprofitable are swiftly replaced in hopes of making returns on investment. This is how the Nakagin Capsule Tower failed. An unachievable remedy to a systemic issue, the Tower aimed to reorganize the Japanese conception of urban planning through a single building within a country suffering an acute land deficit.

Nonetheless, The Nakagin Capsule Tower’s place in the history of architecture and Japan as a whole should not be undermined because of the death of the building’s physical structure. In response to Kurokawa’s campaign to save the Tower, the global architecture community praised the building for its innovative prefabricated design and unseen take on the lifecycle of architecture.21 The Nakagin Capsule Tower’s design philosophy arguing for the building’s capability to change with its environment remains a constructive thought experiment in the current era as cities continue to exhibit the same cycles Kurokawa feared. More specifically, the building lives on as an expression of how prefabricated architecture could promote an expressive and sustainable lifestyle within one of the most rapidly advancing cultural contexts. The disappearance of the Nakagin Capsule Tower may not denote a failure, but Metabolism’s precise necessity in an exact time and place, an urgent spark of constructive design lit in the uncertainty of Japan’s reconstruction.
