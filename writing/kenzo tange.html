Motorized Mobility Through Idealized Planning—Kenzo Tange’s Plan for Tokyo Bay

The availability, reliability, and mobility offered by public transportation in the modern city have disincentivized the use of automobiles as a means of conveyance; the pitfalls of parking and urban congestion have decayed the efficiency of the automobile. It does not have to be this way, however; maybe it is not the car that is the problem, but instead it is the city that surrounds the car that needs to be refitted. This idea was at the core of Japanese architect Kenzo Tange’s, A Plan for Tokyo Bay. Conceived during the height of Tokyo’s exponential population growth following the destruction of Japan in World War II, Tange’s plan aimed to revitalize Tokyo and equip it with the means to bear a population of over 10,000,000.1 Kenzo Tange believed that communication was the lifeblood of modern society and that mobility through transportation was the enzyme that actuated this process, but the contemporary structure of Tokyo was inhibiting this and crumbling under the weight of its population. The Plan for Tokyo Bay stands as a monument in the ephemeral history of Japanese Metabolist Architecture for its proposed regenerative growth and the biological inspirations for its design, but this paper will not be discussing those aspects; this paper will discuss how Kenzo Tange’s obsession with the automobile drove him to the point where he created an impossible plan for construction. In Kenzo Tange’s Plan for Tokyo Bay, the architect conceptualized a symbolic reimagining of Tokyo where the mobility offered by the automobile was no longer bound by the archaic structure of the city.

“At Present, the City is Confused, Paralyzed, and Moribund”2

Kenzo Tange’s fixation with mobility rested on the notion that mobility through transportation was a catalyst for economic success and Japan’s prosperity. Through a long and somewhat-convoluted train of logic within the first few pages of the pamphlet outlining A Plan for Tokyo, Tange essentially comes to the conclusion that transportation gave impetus to the “function” of Tokyo, as he called it; that is the network of communication that connected working people in an effort to power the economy and thus bolster Japan’s success as a global Capitalist power.3 Transportation was the arterial system that circulated the immense population of working people in Tokyo and thus circulated the information that powered the forward-moving nature of Japan. The continuous flow of information throughout Tokyo connected the many components of the economy; as the many parts collaborated for the benefit of the whole, Japan steadily rebuilt its economy from its postwar state.

With this information, Tange saw that the automobile was the most promising form of transportation, yet the structure of Tokyo itself did not accommodate it. With the capability to deliver information in mind, the automobile was the most optimal form of transportation as it theoretically allowed for free, individual movement.4 However, this movement was constricted by two circumstances—the centripetal flow of movement into Tokyo’s center and the city’s disunion of transportation and architecture. The dilemma of urban sprawl that came with the growing population of Tokyo moved many working people towards the outskirts of the city. As a result, much of the daily automobile traffic moved towards the center of the city—where the pivotal industries were located. The traffic jams and congestion created in the heart of Tokyo due to the convergence of the many commuters effectively paralyzed the flow of information. Furthermore, the ability to park directly in front of one’s destination—the car’s main advantage over mass transportation—was often inhibited by the lack of integrated parking space in Tokyo; the city’s architecture was not designed with the automobile in mind, resulting in a segregation between the road and the building.5 The outdated foundation of the city that Tokyo was built upon, as Tange saw it, was not equipped with the facilities to uphold the fluid motion of automobiles.

Kenzo Tange’s fascination with the utility of the automobile was all but new, and A Plan for Tokyo would become the most concrete realization of Tange’s vision for the future of transportation in Tokyo, as Korean Advanced Institute of Science and Technology Professor Hyunjung Cho argues. Dating back to the wartime period, Tange drafted plans for a high-speed expressway spanning from Tokyo to the base of Mount Fuji as means of reaching a proposed monument.6 This interest in mobility would develop into research on the complicated system of commuting in Tokyo as Tange would become an assistant professor at Tokyo University; his research began with studies on locomotive transportation and eventually culminated into Tange’s seminal 1959 dissertation on the necessary transformation Tokyo must undergo given the contemporary trend of motorisation.7 His dissertation argued for a three-dimensional restructuring of Tokyo’s urban core, featuring underground parking space, public spaces at ground level, and elevated commercial and financial buildings above; this solution stemmed from Tange’s argument that “the increase of automobile traffic requires an innovation of architectural form and a new urban imagery—”a sentiment that would directly manifest in Tange’s radical plan for Tokyo Bay.8

At the 1960 Tokyo World Design Conference, Kenzo Tange would unveil his team’s conception of a model for Tokyo fit to promote the mobility offered by the age of motorization; this proposal would incorporate three tenets into its structure:

l. To shift from a radial centripetal system to a system of linear development

2. To find a means of bringing the city structure, the transportation system, and urban architecture into organic unity

3. To find a new urban spatial order which will reflect the open organization and the spontaneous mobility of contemporary society9

A Plan for Tokyo, 1960 — Toward a Structural Reorganization10

Spanning across the previously-unexplored Tokyo Bay, from Tokyo to Chiba, a network of artificial land-masses concentrated in a linear fashion is the main spectacle of Kenzo Tange’s A Plan for Tokyo. Branching from this main axis, is a series of root-like extensions dotted with tent-shaped edifices. Running through each organ of this city on water is a multi-level, many-laned highway system equipped with roads, monorails, and subway systems (see Figure 1.). Extending Tokyo over the water seems entirely incongruous, but Tange believed that doing so was the only option. The city’s increasing population made the already small proportion of habitable land exorbitantly-expensive; building over the bay would avoid expending this limited resource and instead open a new pathway towards amphibious urban planning.11 This first step onto the water would give Tange the freedom to draft a solution to the problems that inhibited the mobility necessary to sustain a city like Tokyo.

The linear “Civic Axis” of Tange’s plan was conceived in direct response to the archaic nature of the radial conception of a city. The status quo saw Tokyo’s governing functions—the many buildings associated with the three sectors of the economy—concentrated at the heart of the city. Tange saw this model as best fit for a time before the automobile—the time of “horses and carriages.”12 By 1960, the volume of movement throughout the city thanks to the automobile was no longer being supported by this model as traffic congealed towards the center. To circumvent this flaw, the architect distributed Tokyo’s pivotal functions across Tokyo Bay in a linear fashion. Governmental, financial, research, and consumer-focused buildings would be arranged in sections according to their sectors and places accordingly along the Civic Axis.13 Tange backed up this spatial reorganization with an analogy; much like a conveyor belt, the work performed by the many working people of Tokyo—the transmission of information from sector to sector—would be “unified by the linear movement” of the Civic Axis.14 As traffic moves down the axis, it remains uninhibited as there is no room for convergence in a linear road system. The intentionally simple nature of the Civic Axis would, in fact, be the most efficient organization of Tokyo.

Kenzo Tange’s Civic Axis, as University of Pennsylvania Associate Professor and Metabolist scholar Zhongjie Lin proposes, was the antithesis of another increasingly-popular model for cities following World War II—The Garden City. Exercises in urban planning were being carried out to mitigate the urban sprawl induced by postwar population increase in many of the world’s largest cities. British Town Planner Patrick Abercrombie conceived of a solution in 1944 modeled after the Garden City—the methodology of creating small suburban communities on the outskirts of congested cities. Abercrombie thought to create “a series of new towns on London’s outer rings” to offset the weight endured by London. This model became a popular solution and was even proposed in the case of Tokyo just four years before Tange’s plan.15 Tange, however, believed the creation of “satellite cities” would only “add to the burden of the urban nucleus;” as more workers were placed towards the outer rims of Tokyo, even more traffic would move towards the urban center as the commute would inevitably gravitate towards the governing functions of the city.16

The Civic Axis’ success would only be achieved if it was paired with a transportation system that accommodated the volume and variability of the movement that powered Tokyo.

The one-way, three-dimensional highway system that encircled the Civic Axis was bred to moderate the variable nature of transportation in Tokyo. Based on contemporary population trends, Tange predicted that in 20 years from 1960, Tokyo would have a population of 15,000,000, and some six million of those people would be flooding into the proposed Civic Axis to perform their daily functions.17 In the status quo, a mixed use of mass transit—Tokyo’s subways and monorails—and automobiles would deliver humans into the urban center. This would create a “regular, permanent flow” of locomotive commuters and a “variable flow” of automotive commuters. A system that would sustain the steady, robotic pattern of mass transit while simultaneously stimulating the “organic” flow of automobiles was the inspiration behind the Tokyo Bay Plan’s transit system.18 The layered highway system seen in the plan served this express purpose. Each level of this highway would host ten lanes, and the speed of these lanes would increase up each level; the lowest level would feature a loop every kilometer, the next would feature one every three, and the fastest loop every nine. Along the sections where these loops overlap, ramps would allow for ascent or descent as traffic would run in the same direction as seen in Figure 2. The second level would also feature a monorail.19 This transit system was, in theory, the perfect organism that mitigated congestion and hastened mobility through a natural hierarchy of speeds; this model not only streamlined the described variable flow of automotive traffic but effectively unified it with the underlying, predictable mass transit system.

Amidst the loops of the Tokyo Bay Plan’s transit system, Tange’s proposed unification of transportation and architecture manifested itself in the pilotis and core system structures that hosted the industries of Tokyo. The separation of the building and the street, in Tange’s mind, acted against the free movement enabled by the automobile as one could not actually come to a stop in front of their destination without space for parking. Thus, in designing the buildings that comprise the Civic Axis, Tange sought to integrate the building and street into one entity. Each building in the Civic Axis consisted of three layers (see Figure 3). Each loop on the highway connected to the bottom-most layer of the building they encircled—a parking structure. This level allows commuters to park their cars and ascend to the next level, an open space meant for public activities. Supported by massive tube-like pillars that run through all of these levels, elevated office buildings would house the private business activities of Tokyo’s workers.20 This system was a combination of the pilotis structure, utilized in many of Tange’s constructions, and the novel core system of Tange’s creation. The pilotis is a method of elevating a building above a parking space in order to mesh a building’s internal functions with the traffic coming into it. The core system utilized so-called “arteries—”shafts that acted as support beams, elevator shafts, and service entries—to directly connect the people and power that operate society with the buildings that bolster the economy.21 The combination of these two systems was Tange’s “means for bringing the city structure, the traffic system, and architecture together into an organic whole;” the realms of the street and the building were unified as commuters could come to a stop freely, parking beneath any desired building, and ascend to their workplace.22 From the smallest unit of the Civic Axis to the entire extension over Tokyo Bay, Tange’s idealized reality sought for seamless transition between the two once-divided realities in order to hasten and optimize the potent mobility offered by the automobile in Tokyo.

“A Foundation Stone in the Construction of a New City”23

Despite the eagerness that it was presented with and the years of work preceding it, the construction of Kenzo Tange’s proposed future for Tokyo was, in fact, most likely impossible. The very foundation of his plan was up for scrutiny as writers from Shinkenchiku, an architecture and urbanism publication, pointed out the apparent absence of engineers on Tange’s team and raised concerns about the stability of the man-made earth erected from the sea bed.24 The Civic Axis’ highway system was deemed impractical by British architect Peter Smithson in response to the fact that “all movement must proceed via that spine even when it has no business there;” a ludicrous amount of lanes would be necessary to accommodate the amount of cars forced through one direction.25 Moreso, Tange himself looked at the execution of his plan through a pessimistic lens. In the final portion of his pamphlet, Tange states that “the sectionalism that prevails in the government and in the bureaucracy prevents the birth of any comprehensive policy;” for a plan of such scale to be carried out, a massive governmental authority would be required—an entity not present in postwar Tokyo.26

Kenzo Tange’s A Plan for Tokyo was an impossible accomplishment by design, created in the wake of the impossibility of the problem in Tokyo given the existing methodology and technology. Before revealing the actual plan, Tange quite extensively lists other routes that could have been taken to solve Tokyo’s problems: redistributing industries to satellite cities, dispersing Tokyo’s functions, and revamping Japan’s highways. These plans, despite their greater feasibility, would be ineffective; they would only increase the amount of centripetal traffic given the radial structure of Tokyo.27 This realization is at the heart of Tange’s fantastical imagining of the future of Tokyo. If the problem in Tokyo is the city’s structure itself, a solution that works within these constraints is destined to fail. In creating an impossible plan in spite of existing technologies, Tange attempted to continue the previously-unsuccessful work done to fix Tokyo by creating a solution unlike anything seen so far. This sentiment is echoed in some of Tange’s final words in the pamphlet: “It is impossible to expect the force for change to come from within the existing system and organization.”28

A transposition of the ethos behind the Archigram movement, as described by UC Davis Architecture History Professor Simon Sadler, can be used as a lens to understand that Tange’s ascension from practical architectural form was necessary to achieve his vision. Archigram was a conceptual architectural movement of the 1960’s inspired by the Japanese Metabolist movement; the movement can be loosely-characterized as an array of imaginative plans for mobile, autonomous structures unified under the tenet of mobility powering every aspect of life, from the person to the building.29 But the plans were just that—plans. The technology necessary to power these architectural goliaths was utterly unachievable then and even now. This absurdity was, in fact, an exercise in true architectural creativity as opposed to the rigid, static “vocabulary” of modern architecture that the movement worked against, as Sadler argues; in ignoring the “functionalist rationale” of modernist architecture, Archigram demanded that “every design be born of inspiration.”30 This approach allowed for solutions to problems that were unable to be solved with the set of tools real architecture provided. An understanding of this dilemma further explains Tange’s reliance on symbolic solutions to Tokyo’s problems rather than concrete, calculated ones. In superseding the practical realm of a solution to Tokyo’s problems, Tange effectively pivoted in a new direction; by drafting a solution to a problem that is not technically achievable, one is given the chance to iterate and improve upon a plan spurred from uninhibited inspiration. Eventually, when the technology permits, a functioning solution could follow. And maybe then Tokyo could be saved.

During a symposium presenting the plan, Kenzo Tange participated in a panel with other experts in urban planning. After facing a barrage of critique from his fellow panelists, Tange broke the proceeding silence and established: “everyone agrees that Tokyo needs a solution, and that has to be an unconventional project with fundamentally different approaches and ideas from the urban planning we’ve known. Rather than criticizing the plan, we should find a way to back up the proposal.”31
